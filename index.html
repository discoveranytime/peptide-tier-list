2The reasoning

Every placement on this list is a judgment about the total evidence package — not a declaration that a compound is universally good or bad. Six factors feed each call: the quality of the human evidence, the magnitude and consistency of the effect, the safety profile, how practically useful it is, whether better-established alternatives exist, and how much confidence you can have in what's actually in the vial. Different goals can legitimately produce different rankings for the same compound.

The single most important rule: S-tier means "strongest overall case," not "strongest effect." A modest compound with excellent human evidence can outrank a dramatic one with none — and an effective compound can still land in F if its risks are disproportionate for the use case. Evidence quality carries real weight, which is why long clinical histories and formal approvals count for so much.

Mechanism is not outcome. A compound can genuinely raise a biomarker without that translating into the results people actually want. Animal findings, plausible pathways, and enthusiastic anecdotes are starting points, not conclusions — anecdotes in particular are wildly inconsistent, and what works in a dish or a mouse doesn't automatically work in a person.

These tiers are a snapshot, not a verdict. Good rankings should move as new evidence appears: a compound with thin human data today could earn a promotion tomorrow on the strength of a well-run trial, and a high flyer can fall if its evidence foundation cracks. Read the list as "where the evidence stands right now" — and argue with it. That's the point.
